This is a Claude session — a creativity session, really — where I was trying to find a brand name for myself. Below is the raw transcript: what I said, what Claude said, and the name we landed on by the end. After the transcript, I break down the concepts and frameworks that were quietly at play throughout the conversation — from conversation repair to trademark law to sound symbolism — with links back to where each one comes from.

## The conversation

**Mario:** Hey. Um, I'm about to create a brand name, and I need your help. So for now, what I have so far is the letters x, y, and I'm thinking about changing that to Mario without the o. And reason being my aunt was named Mario when instead of the o was an a, and I want to play homage to her.

**Claude:** Hey Mario. That's a really lovely idea — turning "Maria" into your brand name as a tribute like that has a lot of heart. Just to make sure I've got it: you're thinking "Maria" spelled M-A-R-I-A, dropping the final A... so it'd read as "Mari"? Or did you mean something else by "Mario without the o"?

**Mario:** Yeah, Marie.

**Claude:** Got it, so it'd be spelled M-A-R-I. Which pronunciation did you have in mind, and is this replacing the X-Y idea entirely or is it for a different brand?

**Mario:** I think I don't care about the pronunciation at this stage as long as I have a good reason to have it, and as long as it tells me it's an okay name, like Odin, like Nike. It is not meant to replace X-Y, but it might because X-Y is a very short and generic name, and I might have problems having to dedicate that name to me. But Mari — that's what I'm trying to do, trying to see if it's going to be easier for me to have this as a name instead of X-Y.

**Claude:** *(checked notes on the XY / holding-level concept)* Ahh, okay, this makes sense now. So "Mari" would actually be for the big holding-level concept, the one currently called "X Y," the technology-first agency successor idea, not for Plumbeer.

Here's my honest read: Odin and Nike work because they're short, evocative, and don't explain themselves. They lean on myth and feeling rather than description. "Mari" could genuinely sit in that same category — warm, human, easy to say, and it doesn't box you into one niche the way "X Y" or something literal would. The personal tribute to your aunt also gives you a real story to tell, which matters a lot for brand narrative, more than X Y's "ask why" explanation does.

The tradeoff: X Y is more overtly conceptual for a tech agency positioning, while Mari is warmer and more personal, maybe less obviously "tech" at first glance. Does that distinction matter for how you want this brand to come across?

**Mario:** Yeah, it does, because I also want to make sure I'm seen as a tech-first agency. So what can we do with this name such that it comes clear? For example, can you put something at the end or at the beginning?

**Claude:** Interesting instinct. Something like "Mari X" could work — the X adding a technical, almost algorithmic edge while Mari keeps the warmth and the story. Or you could flip it to "X Mari." Do you want me to think through a few more variations, or riff on taglines that would make the tech-first positioning explicit alongside the name?

**Mario:** No, the tagline I can work out afterwards. I think the name for now is what matters. So if you tell me Mari is fine, we can start with that.

**Claude:** I'd say Mari on its own is fine, but if the tech-first signal really matters to you, Mari X gives you both things at once — the personal story and the technical edge — without losing what makes it work. Either way, I think it clears the bar: short, real story behind it, doesn't trap you in one niche, which was your worry with X Y anyway. Want me to save this as the direction you're going with?

**Mario:** So what about MariOS?

**Claude:** Oh, that's clever. MariOS, playing off "operating system," ties directly into your "new creativity operating system" concept. It's got the tribute to your aunt, it screams tech, and it's memorable. Genuinely, I think that might be your strongest option so far. One thing worth flagging: it sounds very close to Mario, the video game, and also your own name. Does that feel like a fun coincidence to lean into, or a bit too close for comfort?

**Mario:** Yeah, it was on purpose, right? So I think it's fine. So if you tell me MariOS is better than X-Y and better than Mari on its own, I'll go with that.

**Claude:** Honestly, yeah — I think it edges out both. It keeps the personal story, makes the tech-first, operating-system positioning explicit rather than implied, and it's more distinctive and memorable than a bare two-letter name like X Y. I'd go with MariOS.

**Mario:** Please convert this entire session to markdown and let's make sure to tag who said what.

**Claude:** *(this document)*

## The concepts underneath it

Read back closely, the conversation wasn't just improvisation — it was tracing a handful of well-studied frameworks from linguistics, cognitive psychology, and brand theory, mostly without either of us naming them at the time. Here's what was actually going on turn by turn.

### Tribute naming and eponymous branding

The whole session starts from a single move: taking a real person's name — my aunt Maria — and turning it into a commercial mark. This is a well-documented category in onomastics (the study of names) and brand-history writing, where trade names are traced back to founders, family members, or people the founder wanted to honor. Adrian Room's [*Dictionary of Trade Name Origins*](https://search.worldcat.org/title/7812164) catalogs hundreds of examples of exactly this pattern. It resurfaces later in the conversation too, when I confirm that echoing my own name in "MariOS" was intentional — the same self-referential, eponymous instinct applied to myself instead of my aunt.

### Repair: how we untangled the spelling

The first few exchanges — Claude asking "M-A-R-I-A, dropping the final A?", me saying "Yeah, Marie," Claude asking again — is a textbook example of what conversation analysts call a *repair sequence*: one speaker flags a possible misunderstanding and the pair works, turn by turn, to resolve it before the conversation can move forward. This mechanism was formally described by Schegloff, Jefferson, and Sacks in their landmark paper, ["The Preference for Self-Correction in the Organization of Repair in Conversation"](https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.1977.0041) (*Language*, 1977). Their finding that repair sequences often stack — one clarification triggering another — is exactly what happens here, across three consecutive turns, before the name "Mari" is settled.

### Satisficing: good enough, not perfect

When I said I didn't care about pronunciation "as long as I have a good reason to have it," that's a small but clean example of *satisficing* — a decision-making strategy where you stop searching once an option clears a "good enough" threshold, rather than exhaustively optimizing every variable. The term comes from Herbert Simon's ["Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment"](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042769) (*Psychological Review*, 1956), which argued that real decision-makers, bounded by time and information, rarely optimize — they satisfice. The same pattern shows up again later when I explicitly deferred the tagline decision until after the name was locked: prioritizing the higher-order decision and setting the lower-order one aside.

### Odin, Nike, and the trademark distinctiveness spectrum

Citing Odin and Nike as the bar to clear wasn't arbitrary — both are what trademark law calls *fanciful* or *arbitrary* marks: words with no inherent descriptive connection to the product or service they represent. This is the strongest, most legally protectable end of what's known as the "spectrum of distinctiveness," first laid out by the U.S. Second Circuit in [*Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc.*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/537/4/468363/), 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976). A name that doesn't explain itself, like Nike or Odin, has to earn its meaning entirely through association — which is precisely the bet being made on "Mari" and later "MariOS."

### Sound symbolism and brand narrative

Claude's read on why Odin and Nike "work" — short, evocative, don't explain themselves — echoes research on *sound symbolism* in branding: the finding that the phonetic qualities of a name (independent of literal meaning) shape how people perceive a brand, studied extensively by Richard Klink in ["Creating Meaningful New Brand Names: A Study of Semantics and Sound Symbolism"](https://doi.org/10.1080/10696679.2001.11501889) (*Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice*, 2001). Alongside that, the appeal to "a real story to tell" draws on *brand narrative* theory — the idea that a brand accrues meaning through the relationship and story a consumer can attach to it, not just its functional attributes. This was formalized by Susan Fournier in ["Consumers and Their Brands: Developing Relationship Theory in Consumer Research"](https://doi.org/10.1086/209515) (*Journal of Consumer Research*, 1998).

### Positioning and morphological branding

When I asked how to make the tech-first signal explicit, that's a direct instance of *positioning* — deliberately shaping how a name signals its category and competitive space in an audience's mind, rather than leaving it to be inferred. The concept is essentially the foundation of Al Ries and Jack Trout's [*Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Positioning-:-the-battle-for-your-mind/oclc/6487734) (1981). Claude's proposed fix — appending "X" to get "Mari X" or "X Mari" — is a case of *morphological branding via affixation*: attaching a short, semantically loaded unit to an existing name to shift its perceived category, a technique studied by Klink in a later paper, ["Creating Meaningful Brands: The Relationship Between Brand Name and Brand Mark"](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1027476132607) (*Marketing Letters*, 2003).

### Lexical blending: how "MariOS" was built

"MariOS" itself is a *portmanteau* — a lexical blend that fuses "Mari" and "OS" into a single new word that carries both source meanings at once. Blending as a deliberate, meaning-preserving word-formation process (distinct from accidental slips or simple compounding) is analyzed by Elke Ronneberger-Sibold in ["Lexical Blends: Functionally Tuning the Transparency of Complex Words"](https://doi.org/10.1515/flin.40.1-2.155) (*Folia Linguistica*, 2006). It's the same mechanism behind names like "brunch" or "podcast" — two recognizable pieces fused so that both stay legible inside the new word.

### The Mario echo: mere exposure

Claude flagging that "MariOS" sounds close to Mario — my own name and the video-game character — surfaces the *mere-exposure effect*: the finding that repeated or familiar-sounding stimuli tend to generate more favorable attitudes, simply through familiarity. It was established by Robert Zajonc in ["Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure"](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025848) (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1968). Whether that familiarity helps or hurts a brand name depends on whether the echo is deliberate — which, as I confirmed, it was.

### Brand equity as the final filter

The closing evaluation — is MariOS more distinctive and memorable than "X Y" or "Mari" alone — applies the same criterion throughout the whole session: differentiation and recall as the two variables that actually build brand strength. This is the core thesis of Kevin Lane Keller's ["Conceptualizing, Measuring, and Managing Customer-Based Brand Equity"](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299305700101) (*Journal of Marketing*, 1993), which treats brand equity as a function of how distinct and how easily recalled a brand is in a consumer's mind. That's ultimately the test MariOS was measured against, and the one it passed.

## Where else this applies

None of these concepts are branding-specific — naming just happens to be where they collided in this conversation. Five of the most widely known:

- **Brand equity (distinctiveness + memorability)** — a general checklist for evaluating any name, title, or label before you commit to it.
- **Mere-exposure effect** — UI/UX familiarity design, marketing repetition strategy, or choosing names that intentionally echo something already well-known.
- **Positioning** — messaging strategy for a product, a resume, or a personal brand — deciding what category you want to be judged against.
- **Satisficing** — any decision under time pressure: picking a vendor, a hire, a design direction — set a "good enough" bar instead of chasing the optimum.
- **Trademark distinctiveness spectrum** — evaluating *any* new name (product, company, feature) for how legally protectable and ownable it will be.
